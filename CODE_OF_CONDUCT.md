# MeaningWire Code of Conduct

MeaningWire welcomes participation from people with different backgrounds, disciplines, experience levels, and viewpoints. Technical rigor and respectful collaboration are both required.

## Expected behavior

Participants should:

- discuss ideas and evidence rather than attacking people;
- make room for questions, uncertainty, and correction;
- distinguish fact, interpretation, hypothesis, and preference;
- give credit for others' work and sources;
- disclose relevant conflicts of interest;
- respect privacy and security boundaries;
- avoid harassment, threats, discriminatory language, sexualized conduct, or deliberate humiliation;
- accept reasonable maintainer moderation intended to keep the project useful and safe.

Strong disagreement is welcome when it is specific, evidence-based, and directed at the work rather than the person.

## Unacceptable behavior

Examples include:

- targeted harassment or intimidation;
- discriminatory or demeaning comments;
- publishing another person's private information without permission;
- sustained disruption of project discussions;
- knowingly misrepresenting another contributor's work, credentials, or statements;
- using project channels primarily for spam, unrelated promotion, or manipulation.

## Scope

This code applies in project-managed spaces and when someone is acting as a representative of MeaningWire in public project activity.

## Enforcement

Concerns should be reported privately to the project maintainer when possible. Until a dedicated project contact address is published, use GitHub's private reporting or moderation mechanisms where available rather than posting sensitive details publicly.

If no private path is available, a public issue may be used only to request private moderator contact. Do not place sensitive personal information, allegations requiring confidentiality, security details, or private evidence in that public request.

Maintainers may remove content, limit participation, or block contributors when necessary to protect the project and its participants. Enforcement decisions should be proportionate, documented when appropriate, and focused on behavior rather than status or viewpoint.

## Good-faith correction

People make mistakes. Prompt acknowledgment, correction, and changed behavior should generally be preferred over punishment when risk is low and the conduct is not severe or repeated.
